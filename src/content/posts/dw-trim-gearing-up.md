Quick one. [The anchor is locked](/posts/dw-trim-anchor/) — the bar-and-restaurant buildout for the chain — and this week is about turning a signature into a mobilized job. The gearing-up phase is where well-run jobs are actually won; the build itself just records the result.

Three things getting done before the first piece of trim goes up:

**Materials against the takeoff, not the estimate.** The bid had a six-line sheet and a real quantity takeoff behind it — the kind that flipped several line items from the first pass and priced the job honestly. Ordering comes off the takeoff. The estimate got the job; the takeoff builds it. Wainscot scope gets field-confirmed before the sheet order, not after.

**The schedule is a bid line.** Days-on-site was priced like a material. So the calendar gets the same respect as the lumber order: sequence decided up front, punch-list time reserved before it's needed, and no heroic compression mid-job. Chains don't watch your craftsmanship half as closely as they watch your schedule.

**Paperwork the day it happens.** Site photos, delivery receipts, change conversations — logged same-day, every day. Not because anyone's auditing this job, but because the next five sites inherit whatever documentation discipline this one builds. The anchor job's real product is the template for the other five.

Short post, short phase. Then the build goes loud and the updates get sparser. That's what winning looks like from the inside: boring, sequenced, documented.
