DW Trim locked the anchor job. A bar-and-restaurant buildout for a popular chain — the kind of project that isn't really one project. One site signed, five more builds queued behind it, and more bids out beyond all six.

That's the whole shape of commercial trim work: chains don't test you twice. They hand you one location and watch. Run it clean — schedule, tolerance, punch list — and the other five come with it. Stumble and the pipeline closes just as automatically.

## Why the anchor matters more than the number

The contract value of one buildout is fine. That's not the point. The point is that this job prices the next five: the crew's pace on this floor plan becomes the estimate for the next one, the sub relationships sharpened here carry over, and the chain's facilities people make their repeat-or-not call on this single site.

So the plan is narrow on purpose. Hands-on where it counts — the details that get walked on the punch list — and delegation everywhere else. No new systems mid-job. No heroics. Finish clean, document everything, and let the work sell the chain.

## The pipeline math

- 1 signed (this one)
- 5 more builds behind it in the chain
- More bids out beyond all six

That's a runway measured in quarters, not weeks. Eyes on Q1 '27 for where this lands — the anchor runs through the back half of this year, and the follow-on sites pace out from its completion.

The [bid takeover post](/posts/bbq-bid-takeover/) ended with work taken back into my own hands mid-bid. This is the other half of that lesson: taking work over is a tactic. Landing the anchor is a strategy. Both were needed, and only one of them pays out six times.
